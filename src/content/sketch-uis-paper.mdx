## The Trap of Pixel Perfection

Figma is a dangerous tool. It is *too* good. When you open Figma, you immediately start worrying about 8px grids, auto-layout constraints, and color variables. You skip the "What" and jump straight to the "How."

This premature optimization kills ideas. You become a pixel-pusher instead of a solution-architect.

## The Fidelity Gap

Paper has a feature that no software has: **Low Fidelity by Default**.

When I draw a square on a napkin, it's obviously not a final button. It’s a placeholder. It represents "Action Goes Here." Because it looks messy, my brain feels safe to discard it. I can crumple the paper and draw a new one in 2 seconds.

In software, deleting a perfectly styled component feels like a loss. We fall in love with our execution before we've validated the concept.

## Thinking with the Hand

There is a cognitive loop between the hand and the brain. Sketching is a thinking process.

For my recent project, **OfficeHub**, the entire canvas interface was born on the back of a receipt. I drew circles and arrows connecting them. I realized the hierarchy wasn't a tree; it was a graph.

If I had started in code, I would have built a `Treeview` component because that's what UI libraries provide. Paper allowed me to invent a `Canvas` view because I wasn't constrained by `<divs>`.

## The Hybrid Workflow

My workflow today looks like this:
1.  **Paper**: Chaos phase. rapid iteration. Arrows, scribbles, notes. Find the core interaction.
2.  **Whiteboard**: Clean up the logic. Validate user flows.
3.  **Code**: Skip Figma if possible. Go straight to Tailwind.

I often find that Figma is an unnecessary middleman for solo developers. If you know your design system well, code is faster than detailed mocking. But *nothing* is faster than a pen.

## Conclusion

Don't let your tools dictate your thoughts. Computers are rigid; they demand structure. But ideas are fluid. Catch them on paper first, before they solidify into DOM nodes.
